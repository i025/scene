Reference inventory and parameterization:
Before building, record a short reference inventory in program comments: major visible
objects, approximate normalized image bounds, distinctive visible parts, overlaps, and
uncertain or occluded regions. These are estimates from the current input, not calibration.
Map each inventory item to a named SceneAct asset with meaningful child parts. A valid
hierarchy verifies only the parts emitted; it does not establish photographic completeness.
Do not invent hidden details to fill uncertainty or omit visible parts because they are small.
Keep each object's local dimensions and shape parameters separate from its placement,
orientation and instance scale. Construct reusable assets in a consistent local frame, then
place their grouped subtrees with explicit pivots. Preserve independent editable instances,
semantic child parts and support relations. Keep camera parameters separate from geometry.

Local shape, projected size and depth:
For each visually important composite, estimate its overall contour, component proportions,
occupied space and gaps before adding fine repeated detail. Record a few visible landmarks
or contour ratios inside its estimated image bounds, such as transitions in width, attachment
points, rim position, opening shape and the visible interior fraction. Choose landmarks
supported by the image; do not require uncertain or occluded boundaries to be measurable.
A matching bounding box does not establish a matching profile, internal density or opening.

Treat local profile, overall scale, depth, orientation and camera as distinct parameter
families. Uniform scaling cannot repair an incorrect part-to-whole ratio. Moving an object
along a camera ray changes its projected size and may resemble scaling; one image usually
does not resolve that ambiguity. Use neighboring overlaps, support relationships, repeated
instances and perspective anchors as additional constraints, without claiming unique depth.
If bounding placement is reasonable but the contour or internal ratios differ, first consider
local profile parameters. If contour ratios agree but the footprint differs, consider scale
and depth together with support and occlusion evidence. Inspect orientation before changing
geometry when an opening or thin surface has the wrong apparent aspect or exposure.

Represent a clearly visible cavity or opening with geometry that admits the required view
of its inner surface. A flat cap is appropriate only when it explains the observed appearance;
it cannot generally substitute for a recessed interior or open shell. Keep outer profile,
rim thickness, opening dimensions and interior depth independently adjustable when visible.
Use a small number of supported solids and Boolean operations. Avoid coincident surfaces,
accidental filled openings and decorative complexity that does not affect the visible shape.
Do not automatically add hollowness to objects whose interiors are not evidenced.

Camera and local geometry remain competing explanations. Compare multiple independent
anchors spanning the image and depth range: major boundary lines, object centers, relative
sizes and overlap order. Shared projection discrepancies justify considering a camera change;
a local contour discrepancy justifies considering local shape. Neither pattern proves a cause.
Focal length primarily changes magnification; camera rotation changes framing and projected
orientation; camera position changes viewing direction relative to objects and parallax.
Rotation alone does not reproduce a different viewpoint into a cavity. Do not compensate
for every local mismatch by changing the camera or for every shared mismatch by moving objects.
Repeated objects provide useful consistency constraints, but their apparent differences may
come from perspective, occlusion, orientation or real variation; do not force equal image sizes.

Execution cost planning:
The entire solve has three model calls, two Blender executions and 600 seconds. Use the
reported remaining budget when deciding whether another build and review are possible.
A short Python loop can generate thousands of Blender objects before any image exists.
Plan a compact complete first build so realization and the runner's render leave time for
review. Do not change the runner's resource policy or rendering settings to evade this budget.
Use geometry complexity as the controllable cost.

Count these expansions, not just named assets. Aim initially for a few hundred primitive
meshes across the scene as a planning heuristic, not a runtime guarantee or fixed limit.
Allocate geometry according to visible pixel size, contour, openings, gaps and occlusion.
Prioritize a few adjustable shape-defining parts over dense decoration on an inaccurate form.
If estimates reach thousands, simplify repeated microstructure while retaining inventory
